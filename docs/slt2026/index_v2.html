TL;DR

Brazilian Portuguese TTS voices speak an accent that belongs to no region, and that inconsistency alone — read at three phonological markers — separates synthetic from natural speech at 6.09% EER with an entire TTS provider and an entire natural corpus held out.

Overview

Leading commercial and open-source Text-to-Speech models do not reproduce the regional phonetic diversity of Brazilian Portuguese. By aggregating disparate dialects into a single training distribution, they generate a “diluted” accent: a phonetic profile that tries to represent all regional distributions at once and ends up carrying phonological ambiguity dissociated from natural sociophonetic processes. This work extracts phoneme-level features at consonantal and vocalic realizations with high geographic variance, by combining multilingual phone recognizers with classical signal processing. The distributional gap over these features is large enough to separate natural from synthetic voices with an unsupervised Kernel Density Estimator, and the same interpretable, low-dimensional features are useful on Brazilian Portuguese anti-spoofing benchmarks.

This page collects the material referenced from the paper: the synthesis recipes and prompt texts, the full experimental setup, additional feature distributions, the per-held-out-group breakdown of the cross-dataset experiment, and the accuracy and clustered statistics behind the within-utterance switching analysis. Revision 2 adds what the protocol isolates and what it does not — text, speakers, providers, corpora and the recording channel.

Research Questions & Answers

Can accent inconsistency separate synthetic from natural speech without a supervised detector?

Yes. A Kernel Density Estimator fitted only on the 364 natural speakers flags synthetic voices as out-of-distribution, reaching 93.9% AUROC on a transductive t-SNE 2D projection and 74.3% on a deployable inductive Isomap 15D projection (72.1% for PCA 5D). No synthetic sample is seen during fitting.

What exactly differs, and is it interpretable?

Synthetic voices show shifted sibilant realizations at /s/ coda (Cliff’s δ = +0.77), a distorted spectral shape at /r/ coda (δ = +0.66), a narrower and displaced /o/–/ɔ/ vowel space, and more within-utterance realization switching for /r/ coda and /d,t/ palatalization (δ = +0.49 and +0.55 per utterance). The feature distributions and the switching statistics below extend the paper’s Figures 3 and 4.

Are the features useful on consolidated anti-spoofing benchmarks?

On BRSpeech-DF official splits the best configuration reaches 2.00% EER, against 30.67% for the SLS-ECAPA baseline. On FakeBRAccent under speaker-disjoint cross-validation it reaches 8.0 ± 0.8% EER. Under double leave-one-dataset-out, where an entire TTS group and an entire natural corpus are held out simultaneously, the overall EER is 6.09%, and 8.13% for the fully interpretable feature set.

Where does the approach lose ground?

On corpora built entirely from voice cloning. A cloning model follows the accent realizations of its target speaker, which narrows the variability the method relies on and makes accent-based disambiguation harder than for the general-purpose, mixed-identity voices found in commercial TTS defaults. The three markers also do not disambiguate every Brazilian variety on their own.

What the protocol isolates, and what it does not

The method is only interesting if the separation it measures is the linguistic phenomenon and not the dataset, the recording chain, the text or a synthesis artifact. Before the controls, what is actually being compared.

What the study builds, and what it compares

There is no off-the-shelf corpus for this question, so the synthetic side is constructed here and the natural side is assembled from public corpora. Both are reduced to the same object — one feature vector per voice — before anything is trained.

1 Build the synthetic side. 57 TTS voices — 39 commercial, 18 open-source — each read the same 50 phonetically balanced sentences, giving 2850 utterances at 16 kHz mono. Holding the text fixed across all 57 is what makes voices comparable to each other.
2 Assemble the natural side. 364 speakers drawn from public Brazilian Portuguese corpora — podcasts, sociolinguistic interviews, read speech and crowdsourced prompts — up to 40 utterances each, chosen for dialect and recording-chain variety, never by sociolinguistic label.
3 Reduce both to one vector per voice. A phone recognizer locates every /s/ coda, /r/ coda and /d,t/ in each clip; spectral moments, phone posteriors and formants are read at those positions only, then averaged over that speaker’s clips. A natural speaker and a TTS voice end up as the same kind of object.
4 Decide, on voices never seen in training. Five experiments do this, each holding out something different.

Text

The synthesis text is held fixed and phonetically balanced, the comparison is made on per-speaker averages rather than sentence by sentence, features are read only at the markers, and the reference benchmarks bring text of their own. A text effect would have to survive all four.

The four text controls, in full
MitigationWhat it does
Phonetically balanced synthesis texts The 50 sentences every voice reads are a greedy, phonetically balanced subset of the 1000-sentence Alcaim reading list, selected to minimize the L1 distance between the subset’s phone-unit distribution and that of the full list (final L1 = 0.0115, covering 39 of 44 unit types). The synthetic side is therefore not concentrated on an unusual phonological profile, which is the shape a text confound would have to take to imitate an accent effect.
Per-speaker averaging Every feature is a mean over that speaker’s clips — up to 40 utterances on the natural side, 50 on the synthetic side. Nothing is compared sentence to sentence, so a text effect would have to survive averaging over dozens of different sentences on both sides.
Marker localization Features are read at detected /s/ coda, /r/ coda and /d,t/ positions rather than over the whole utterance, so what enters the comparison is the realization of a marker, not the composition of the sentence around it.
Independent reference datasets BRSpeech-DF, FakeBRAccent and the MLAAD pt subset carry their own text material, unrelated to the 50 sentences. The interpretable features are evaluated on all three, so a result that depended on those particular sentences would not carry across them.

Different hold-out protocols

An entire TTS provider and an entire natural corpus leave the training set at the same time, and features are averaged per voice before the split, so no clip of a scored voice can reach training through another utterance. For each pair of one held-out TTS group g and one held-out natural corpus F:

train = natural[not F]  +  synthetic[not g]
test  = EER( natural[F] , synthetic[g] )

24 synthetic groups (8 curated TTS providers + 16 MLAAD systems)
 x  7 natural folds
 = 168 held-out cells per feature set   (4536 cells over the 27 feature configurations)
What each hold-out removes, in full
Held outConsequence
The whole TTS group No voice of the held-out provider or MLAAD system appears in any training fold — not a different voice of the same provider, not a different sentence from the same voice. The detector meets an unseen synthesis system.
The whole natural corpus No speaker and no recording of the held-out natural source appears in training, so the detector cannot lean on that source’s speakers, dialect mix or recording characteristics.
Speaker identity Features are averaged per speaker and per voice before the split, so an utterance of a test speaker cannot appear in training through a different clip. The same speaker-disjoint discipline applies to the 5-fold experiments of Tables I–II and to FakeBRAccent, where the same speaker appears in both the bonafide and the spoofed parts and a naive split would leak identity.
Benchmark splits BRSpeech-DF uses its official train/validation/test split. MLAAD pt is synthetic only, so the natural side of those folds always comes from the other sources.

Channel

Every corpus goes through the same front-end before any feature is extracted: 16 kHz mono resampling, silence trim at top_db = 30, RMS normalization to 0.05 with 0.99 peak safety, and per-utterance cepstral mean normalization of the MFCC block. Identical treatment for the natural pool and for MLAAD is the point: MLAAD is the only 22050 Hz source in the study.

That this matters is measured, not assumed. Running the same hold-out protocol on un-normalized audio and then on normalized audio moves the held-out MLAAD EER from near zero — the detector reading the recording chain — to the same range as an unseen commercial provider:

More feature distributions

These panels are descriptive, and their p-values are exploratory. Figure 3 of the paper, and the panels below that extend it, exist to illustrate what differs between natural and synthetic speech at each marker. The features in them were chosen because they separate the two groups — by tree-ensemble importance, as described just below — so the p-values printed on them are computed on the same data that selected them. They describe the contrast inside the panel; they do not test a pre-registered hypothesis, they carry no multiple-comparison correction over the 75 candidate features, and they should not be read as inferential evidence. The inferential claims of the paper rest on the held-out detection results and on the clustered switching analysis, neither of which selects its features on the evaluation data.

Figure 3 of the paper shows three of these panels. The features below are the most discriminative interpretable features for the natural-versus-synthetic contrast, ranked by tree-ensemble importance: the top 12 by mean absolute SHAP value of an XGBoost classifier and the top 12 by RandomForest impurity importance, both fitted on the same 364 natural and 57 synthetic speakers (the two rankings agree with Spearman ρ = 0.72). Each panel is labelled with the rankings that selected it. Importance is multivariate, so a feature can be useful to the classifier in combination with others while showing little separation on its own; two of the panels below are of that kind and carry a small, non-significant δ.

On the effect size. These panels report Cliff’s δ, computed from the Mann-Whitney U statistic as 2U/(mn) − 1, with δ > 0 meaning the synthetic distribution is stochastically larger. The submitted version of Figure 3 printed Cohen’s d in those boxes by mistake; Cliff’s δ is bounded to [−1, 1], and the correct values for the two univariate panels of that figure are δ = +0.77 and +0.66. The vowel panels are bivariate, so they report the Mahalanobis distance D between group centroids with a Hotelling T² p-value instead.

Within-utterance switching: classifier accuracy and clustered statistics

Pseudoreplication. Figure 4 of the submitted version compared utterances as if they were independent draws, when they are repeated measurements on 57 TTS voices and 92 natural speakers. The analysis was re-run clustering by speaker and by voice, and with a cluster bootstrap that resamples voices, speakers, providers and corpora. The hypothesis is preserved for /r/ coda, /d,t/ palatalization and the aggregate, and the figure now reports the clustered statistics; the small /s/-coda effect does not survive and is reported as non-significant. Both the accuracy of the classifiers that produce the metric and the clustered statistics are below.

Figure 4 of the paper measures how often a voice changes its realization of the same marker inside one utterance, using the binary Shannon entropy of the per-utterance mean dialectal probability given by a realization classifier. Two questions about that figure are answered here: how accurate those classifiers are, and what the comparison looks like when utterances are treated as clustered within speakers and voices rather than as independent.

Out-of-fold performance of the classifiers that produce the switching metric, on the human-annotated clips. Leave-one-dataset-out is the stricter protocol: the classifier never sees the held-out source corpus, so it cannot exploit its recording characteristics.

How to read these numbers. All three classifiers beat chance on balanced accuracy (0.72, 0.64 and 0.62 against 0.50, 0.33 and 0.50 under speaker holdout), so each carries real information about the realization. Raw accuracy is a different matter where the classes are skewed: /d,t/ is palatalized in 83% of the annotated clips, and under the stricter dataset holdout the classifier’s raw accuracy (0.81) sits just below that majority rate, because it trades errors on the majority class for gains on the minority one. The switching metric consumes the probability rather than the decision, and the natural and synthetic sides are scored by the same classifier, so a miscalibrated motor affects both sides alike; but the absolute switching values should not be read as exact realization rates. This is the same caveat the project records for the switching protocol in general.

Clustered inference
Why this table exists. The utterances compared in Figure 4 are not independent: they are clustered within 57 TTS voices, drawn from 8 providers, and within 92 natural speakers, drawn from 6 source corpora. A test over utterances therefore overstates precision. Repeating the comparison with one value per voice and per speaker, and with a cluster bootstrap that resamples voices, speakers, providers and source corpora, leaves the conclusion intact and in fact strengthens the effect for /r/ coda, /d,t/ palatalization and the aggregate. The exception is /s/ coda: its small utterance-level effect does not survive clustering, so no significance is claimed for that marker.

Cross-dataset detection, per held-out group

This is the per-held-out-group evidence behind the isolation argument, whose protocol is stated in Different hold-out protocols. The paper’s Table V reports a double leave-one-dataset-out protocol: for every pair of one held-out TTS group and one held-out natural corpus, a detector is trained on everything else and evaluated on that pair. This gives 24 synthetic groups (8 curated TTS providers and 16 MLAAD systems) × 7 natural corpora = 168 held-out cells per feature set.
